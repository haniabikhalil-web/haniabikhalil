Why I Coach

When I was applying to consulting, I had very little guidance. I made mistakes that, with the right advice at the right moment, could easily have been avoided.

After I joined consulting, I encountered a different set of challenges. There were moments where one honest conversation with someone who understood the environment — but was outside the immediate situation — could have helped me see things more clearly.

I did not always have that person.

And over time I learned something else.

Getting into consulting is hard. Staying in consulting can be even harder.

Breaking in requires preparation, persistence and strong interview performance.

Succeeding once you are inside requires a different set of skills: managing expectations, building relationships, navigating staffing, responding to feedback, understanding performance decisions, communicating with senior stakeholders and figuring out how to build a career that is both successful and sustainable.

Many of those lessons are rarely taught explicitly.

That experience shaped how I think about mentorship. If I can help someone avoid an unnecessary mistake, make a better career decision or approach an important opportunity with greater clarity, I want to do it.

I began coaching alongside my consulting work and later became an official interviewer, which gave me the opportunity to understand recruiting and career decisions from both sides of the table.

Sometimes the most useful thing is having someone outside the problem.

It is not another framework or another piece of advice. It is having someone outside the problem to talk it through with — someone who is not your manager, teammate, recruiter or colleague, and therefore does not have an organizational agenda or personal stake in the decision.

That creates space to step away from the immediate pressure, challenge assumptions and look at the situation from another angle.

You might bring me an interview story you are unsure about, a career move you are considering, difficult feedback you received, a choice between two offers, a staffing problem, a conversation you need to have or simply a decision that does not feel obvious.

My role is not necessarily to tell you what to do. It is to stress-test your thinking, identify blind spots, introduce perspectives you may not have considered and help you reach a decision you can stand behind.

How I Approach Coaching

Diagnose before prescribing

The visible problem is not always the real problem. Before jumping to solutions, I try to understand what is actually holding someone back — whether that is positioning, preparation, communication, decision-making or something else entirely.

Stress-test, don't script

I do not believe good coaching is about memorizing frameworks, rehearsing perfect answers or trying to game an interview. I would rather challenge an argument, test an assumption and ask the uncomfortable question before the interviewer, manager or decision-maker does.

Build independence

The goal is not to make someone dependent on a coach. The best coaching should leave people with better judgment, clearer structure and greater confidence to navigate the next decision themselves.
